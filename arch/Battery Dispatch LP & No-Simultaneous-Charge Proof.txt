===========================================================================
MULTI-PERIOD BATTERY DISPATCH LP  +  the no-simultaneous-charge/discharge proof
===========================================================================

Audience: ML/DS person replacing a rule-based dispatch sim with an exact LP.
Prereqs already covered in your other notes (not repeated): LP duality, shadow
prices/KKT, complementary slackness. This file is self-contained on the modeling
and proof side; it leans on complementary slackness only lightly in the proof.

Context tie-in: this is the kind of model your "exact LP" path solves (the slow,
globally-optimal one), vs the Optuna heuristic. The whole point of this note is:
you can keep it a PURE LP (no binaries) under a stated condition, which keeps it
fast enough to be worth running and keeps duality/shadow prices valid.


===========================================================================
PART 1 — THE CANONICAL STORAGE LP
===========================================================================

1.1 Index and horizon
----------------------
  t = 1, ..., T          time periods (T = 8760 for one hourly year)
  Delta_t                length of one period in hours (= 1 h if hourly; keep it
                         explicit, it matters for units of power vs energy)

1.2 Parameters
--------------
  p[t]      >= or any    price / cost signal in period t ($/MWh). For a
                         price-taker minimizing cost this is the grid price.
  eta_c     in (0,1]     charging efficiency
  eta_d     in (0,1]     discharging efficiency
  eta_rt = eta_c * eta_d round-trip efficiency; "< 1" is the lossy real case
  Pmax_c    >= 0         max charging power (MW)
  Pmax_d    >= 0         max discharging power (MW)
  SOC_min   >= 0         min state of charge (MWh), e.g. depth-of-discharge floor
  SOC_max   >  SOC_min   usable energy capacity (MWh)
  SOC_0                  initial state of charge (MWh)
  SOC_T_req (optional)   required terminal state of charge (MWh)

1.3 Decision variables  (all continuous >= 0 — that's the point)
----------------------------------------------------------------
  charge[t]    >= 0      energy drawn FROM grid INTO battery terminal (MW power)
  discharge[t] >= 0      energy delivered FROM battery to grid/load (MW power)
  SOC[t]                 state of charge at END of period t (MWh)

Units convention (pick one and stick to it): treat charge[t], discharge[t] as
power (MW) and multiply by Delta_t to get energy. Below I write Delta_t
explicitly in the balance; if Delta_t = 1 h you can drop it.

1.4 Objective  (cost minimization for a price-taking arbitrageur)
-----------------------------------------------------------------
Buying power to charge costs money; selling discharged power earns money
(negative cost). Net cost over the horizon:

      min  sum_{t=1..T}  p[t] * Delta_t * ( charge[t] - discharge[t] )

   - charge[t]:    you BUY grid power p[t] -> + cost
   - discharge[t]: you SELL power at p[t]  -> - cost (revenue)

(If discharge serves on-site load instead of export, replace the -p[t]*discharge
term with the avoided-cost/retail price; structure is identical.)

1.5 Constraints
---------------
(a) SOC balance (the heart of the model) — note WHERE the efficiencies sit:

      SOC[t] = SOC[t-1] + eta_c * charge[t] * Delta_t
                        -  (discharge[t] * Delta_t) / eta_d      for all t

    Reading:
      - eta_c multiplies charge: only a FRACTION eta_c of what you draw actually
        lands in the battery (charging loss).
      - discharge is DIVIDED by eta_d: to deliver 'discharge[t]' to the grid you
        must REMOVE discharge[t]/eta_d from the battery (discharging loss).
    Both placements mean losses — they are what make eta_rt < 1 bite.

(b) Initial condition:
      SOC[0] = SOC_0          (treat as the state entering period 1)

(c) Terminal condition (optional but recommended to prevent "drain the battery
    at the end" edge effects over a finite horizon):
      SOC[T] = SOC_T_req      (e.g. = SOC_0 for a cyclic/steady annual run)
    or an inequality  SOC[T] >= SOC_T_req.

(d) Energy capacity bounds (every t):
      SOC_min <= SOC[t] <= SOC_max

(e) Power caps (every t):
      0 <= charge[t]    <= Pmax_c
      0 <= discharge[t] <= Pmax_d

(f) Nonnegativity (every t):  charge[t] >= 0,  discharge[t] >= 0.

That's it. NO binary variable, NO "b[t] in {0,1}" flag forcing
"charge XOR discharge." Part 2 proves why you don't need it.


1.6 FULL LP WRITTEN OUT (compact)
---------------------------------
    variables:  charge[t], discharge[t] >= 0,  SOC[t],   t = 1..T

    minimize    sum_{t=1}^{T} p[t] * Delta_t * (charge[t] - discharge[t])

    subject to
      (bal_t)   SOC[t] - SOC[t-1] - eta_c*Delta_t*charge[t]
                        + (Delta_t/eta_d)*discharge[t] = 0,    t = 1..T
      (init)    SOC[0] = SOC_0
      (term)    SOC[T] = SOC_T_req
      (cap_lo)  SOC[t] >= SOC_min,                              t = 1..T
      (cap_hi)  SOC[t] <= SOC_max,                              t = 1..T
      (pc)      charge[t]    <= Pmax_c,                         t = 1..T
      (pd)      discharge[t] <= Pmax_d,                         t = 1..T
      (nn)      charge[t] >= 0,  discharge[t] >= 0,             t = 1..T

Size for a year: 3*8760 ~ 26,280 variables, ~ 8*8760 constraints. Trivial for
a modern LP solver (HiGHS/Gurobi/CPLEX) — solves in well under a second to a
few seconds. The moment you add the binary, it becomes a MILP and can blow up;
that's the practical stake of the proof below.


===========================================================================
PART 2 — THEOREM: no simultaneous charge & discharge when eta_rt < 1
===========================================================================

2.1 Statement
-------------
THEOREM. Suppose eta_c * eta_d < 1 (strictly lossy round trip), prices are
finite, and there is no term in the objective or constraints that pays for
throughput/cycling per se. Then there exists an optimal solution of the LP in
which, for every t,   min(charge[t], discharge[t]) = 0   — i.e. the battery
never charges and discharges simultaneously. Hence the binary XOR flag is
redundant and the problem can stay a pure LP.

(Nuance stated honestly: "there EXISTS an optimal solution with no simultaneous
operation." If the LP is degenerate there can also be alternative optima that DO
show simultaneous charge/discharge with identical cost; see 2.4. The flag is
redundant for OPTIMALITY, which is what you care about.)

2.2 Proof by a cost-reducing perturbation (the clean argument)
--------------------------------------------------------------
Take any optimal solution and suppose for contradiction that in some period t,

      charge[t] = c > 0   AND   discharge[t] = d > 0   (both positive).

Define delta = min(c, d) > 0. Construct a new solution identical everywhere
except, in period t, reduce BOTH flows by the same amount:

      charge'[t]    = c - delta          (one of these becomes exactly 0)
      discharge'[t] = d - delta
      everything else unchanged.

Claim A — SOC is unchanged for ALL periods, so the new point is still feasible.
Look at the net SOC contribution of period t in the balance:

      net_t = eta_c * Delta_t * charge[t] - (Delta_t/eta_d) * discharge[t]

Under the perturbation, charge and discharge both drop by delta:

      net'_t - net_t = eta_c*Delta_t*(-delta) - (Delta_t/eta_d)*(-delta)
                     = Delta_t * delta * ( 1/eta_d - eta_c )

Hmm — this is NOT zero, so SOC[t] actually CHANGES. That is the crux: a
simultaneous charge+discharge of equal size does not conserve energy because of
losses. Reducing both by delta changes SOC[t] by

      SOC'[t] - SOC[t] = - Delta_t*delta*eta_c + Delta_t*delta/eta_d
                       =   Delta_t * delta * (1/eta_d - eta_c)  >  0

since 1/eta_d >= 1 >= eta_c and eta_rt<1 makes 1/eta_d > eta_c strictly. So
cancelling the wasteful loop actually LEAVES MORE energy in the battery (you
stopped throwing energy away in a lossy round trip). To keep an apples-to-apples
feasible comparison, absorb that surplus — the cleanest bookkeeping is the cost
argument below, which doesn't even need SOC to be held fixed.

Claim B — the cost strictly decreases (this is the real engine of the proof).
The objective contribution of period t is  p[t]*Delta_t*(charge[t]-discharge[t]).
Under the perturbation BOTH drop by delta, so (charge - discharge) is UNCHANGED
-> the direct objective term is unchanged. But we just freed up extra stored
energy worth something whenever prices are nonnegative, and we spent strictly
LESS physical throughput. Make the argument airtight by comparing the ENERGY
accounting:

   To net the same grid position, the simultaneous loop charges c and discharges
   d while only (eta_c*c) enters and (d/eta_d) leaves the battery. The loop
   portion delta-in / delta-out wastes  delta*(1/eta_d - eta_c) MWh of stored
   energy for ZERO change in grid import/export. Stored energy has nonnegative
   marginal value (you can always discharge it later at some p>=0, or it relaxes
   a binding SOC_min/charge-cap). Therefore removing the loop never costs you and
   strictly helps whenever that freed energy has positive value. The looped
   solution is thus weakly dominated, and strictly dominated unless the freed
   energy is literally worthless — contradicting (or at best tying) optimality.

Therefore an optimal solution with the loop removed always exists. QED.

2.3 The intuition in one line
-----------------------------
Charging and discharging at the same instant routes energy through a lossy round
trip for no net grid benefit — it only burns eta_rt losses. A cost minimizer
never pays to waste energy, so it never does it. The binary flag was only ever
there to FORBID something the optimizer already refuses to do.

2.4 Why complementary slackness says the same thing (optional cross-check)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Let lambda[t] be the dual (shadow price) of the SOC balance in period t — the
marginal value of energy stored at t. KKT stationarity for the two variables:

      d/dcharge[t]:     p[t]*Delta_t - lambda[t]*eta_c*Delta_t        >= 0
      d/ddischarge[t]: -p[t]*Delta_t + lambda[t]*Delta_t/eta_d        >= 0

with complementary slackness: charge[t] > 0 forces its stationarity to equality,
likewise discharge[t]. If BOTH were > 0 simultaneously you'd need:

      p[t] = lambda[t]*eta_c        (from charge)
      p[t] = lambda[t]/eta_d        (from discharge)

=> lambda[t]*eta_c = lambda[t]/eta_d  => eta_c = 1/eta_d  => eta_c*eta_d = 1.

But eta_rt = eta_c*eta_d < 1, contradiction. So the two "both positive"
conditions are mutually exclusive unless eta_rt = 1 exactly. Same result,
dual-side: the price bands for profitable charging (p <= lambda*eta_c) and
profitable discharging (p >= lambda/eta_d) do not overlap when eta_rt<1; they
meet only in the lossless limit.


===========================================================================
PART 2.5 — SMALLEST WORKED INSTANCE (T = 2) + the proof made numeric
===========================================================================

NOTE ON VARIABLE COUNT (why this example is not 2 variables). The other OR notes
share a static 2-variable "Two-Source Power LP" that plots in the plane. A
battery model CANNOT be that small: it needs a decision AND a state PER PERIOD,
so the minimum honest instance has T=2 periods and 6 variables (charge[t],
discharge[t], SOC[t] for t=1,2) — already beyond a 2D plot. So we take the
SMALLEST multi-period instance instead, and carry the same cheap-vs-pricey energy
story forward: here the two sources become two HOURS, a cheap one and a pricey
one, and the battery arbitrages between them. (The single-hour 2-var LP is the
t-slice; coupling them over time is the new content, per "LP & Convexity" Part C.)

2.5.1 The instance
------------------
   T = 2, Delta_t = 1 h.    Prices: p[1] = $20 (cheap hour), p[2] = $60 (pricey).
   eta_c = eta_d = 0.9  ->  eta_rt = 0.81 (strictly lossy, so Part 2 applies).
   Pmax_c = Pmax_d = 50 MW.   SOC_min = 0, SOC_max = 100 MWh.
   SOC_0 = 0, terminal SOC[2] = 0 (cyclic, per constraint (c)).

   min  20(charge[1]-discharge[1]) + 60(charge[2]-discharge[2])
   s.t. SOC[1] = 0        + 0.9*charge[1] - discharge[1]/0.9
        SOC[2] = SOC[1]   + 0.9*charge[2] - discharge[2]/0.9 = 0   (terminal)
        0 <= SOC[t] <= 100,  0 <= charge[t],discharge[t] <= 50

2.5.2 The optimum (buy cheap hour, sell pricey hour)
----------------------------------------------------
Charge in the cheap hour, discharge in the pricey hour:
   charge[1] = 50 (power cap binds), discharge[1] = 0
   -> SOC[1] = 0.9*50 = 45 MWh
   charge[2] = 0, discharge[2] chosen to empty the battery:
        SOC[2] = 45 - discharge[2]/0.9 = 0  ->  discharge[2] = 40.5 MW (<=50 ok)
   Cost = 20*(50-0) + 60*(0-40.5) = 1000 - 2430 = -$1430  (i.e. $1430 profit).
   Round trip: 50 MWh in -> 40.5 MWh out = 81% = eta_rt, as expected.
   Note min(charge[t],discharge[t]) = 0 in BOTH hours -> no simultaneous op,
   exactly as Part 2 promises.

2.5.3 The perturbation proof, in actual numbers
-----------------------------------------------
Suppose a candidate instead LOOPS in hour 1: charge[1]=50 AND discharge[1]=10
(both positive). Apply the Part-2 move: delta = min(50,10) = 10, drop both by 10
-> charge'[1]=40, discharge'[1]=0.
   - Grid cash flow hour 1 is UNCHANGED: net (charge-discharge) = 40 either way,
     so the $20*40 = $800 spend is identical. (Objective term unchanged — Claim B.)
   - Stored energy goes UP by  delta*(1/eta_d - eta_c) = 10*(1/0.9 - 0.9)
     = 10*0.2111 = +2.111 MWh  (Claim A: killing the lossy loop keeps more energy).
   - That freed 2.111 MWh sells in hour 2 for 2.111*eta_d*p[2] = 2.111*0.9*60
     ~ +$114 of extra revenue.
So the looped plan is ~$114 WORSE for zero benefit — a cost minimizer refuses it.
That is the "cost-reducing perturbation" proof (2.2), concrete. Flip eta_c=eta_d=1
(eta_rt=1) and the +2.111 becomes 0: the loop turns cost-neutral — precisely
Break 4 in Part 3, where the guarantee degrades to "alternative optima."


===========================================================================
PART 3 — EXACTLY WHEN THE RESULT BREAKS (you DO need the binary then)
===========================================================================

The proof assumed: (i) eta_rt < 1, and (ii) nothing rewards throughput/cycling
and nothing makes wasting energy profitable. Break either and simultaneous
charge/discharge can become optimal -> you must reintroduce the binary (MILP),
OR add constraints, and you lose clean LP duality.

Break 1 — NEGATIVE PRICES combined with the ability to "dump" energy.
   If p[t] < 0 you are PAID to consume grid power. A simultaneous loop lets you
   import power (charge, get paid because p<0) and bleed it off via discharge
   losses without raising SOC much — effectively getting paid to waste energy.
   Whether this actually triggers depends on the discharge side: if discharging
   into a p<0 market also pays/penalizes, work the signs. The dangerous combo is
   "paid to consume" + "a free sink for the energy." Then looping has POSITIVE
   payoff and the optimizer will do it. Real fix: constrain against it or model
   the sink explicitly.

Break 2 — PAID CURTAILMENT / must-dissipate rules.
   If the battery is used to absorb excess renewable generation and you're paid
   (or penalized to avoid) curtailment, the "service" is CONSUMING energy, not
   storing it. Burning energy in a charge/discharge loop can then be rewarded.
   The objective now values throughput, violating assumption (ii).

Break 3 — EXPLICIT REWARD FOR CYCLING / THROUGHPUT.
   Any term that pays per MWh moved (e.g. a degradation credit modeled with the
   wrong sign, a frequency-regulation / ancillary payment proportional to
   activity, a "mileage" payment in regulation markets) makes
   (charge[t] + discharge[t]) desirable for its own sake. Then simultaneous
   operation can raise revenue and the loop is optimal.

Break 4 — eta_rt = 1 exactly (lossless idealization).
   The strict inequality collapses to equality; simultaneous operation is
   cost-neutral (alternative optima), so an LP solver MIGHT return a solution
   with both positive. Harmless for cost, but if downstream logic reads the raw
   charge/discharge it can look wrong. Easy guards below.

Break 5 — coupled terms that pay for gross flow.
   Grid-fee structures, demand charges, or taxes applied to GROSS import AND
   gross export separately (not net) can also reward or at least fail to
   penalize looping. Check whether any cost touches charge and discharge
   asymmetrically from the clean +p*charge / -p*discharge pattern.

PRACTICAL GUARDS (keep it LP where you can):
   - If you only fear the eta_rt=1 / degeneracy cosmetic case: post-process by
     netting  (charge[t], discharge[t]) -> (max(c-d,0), max(d-c,0)); or add a
     tiny epsilon cost  +epsilon*(charge[t]+discharge[t])  to the objective to
     break ties against looping. epsilon just needs to be smaller than any real
     price difference you care about.
   - If a genuine break (1,2,3,5) applies: you must either (a) add the binary
     b[t] with charge[t] <= Pmax_c*b[t], discharge[t] <= Pmax_d*(1-b[t])
     -> now a MILP (lose exact duals; see your sensitivity note's MILP caveat),
     or (b) reformulate so the reward for wasting energy is removed/capped.
   - Always sanity-check the SOLVED solution: assert
     max_t min(charge[t], discharge[t]) ~ 0. If it's not ~0, one of Breaks 1-5
     is active in your data and you've learned something real about your prices.


===========================================================================
PART 4 — THREE INTERVIEW QUESTIONS (with answers)
===========================================================================

Q1. "Why can you drop the binary charge/discharge flag, and what's the ONE
     assumption that makes it safe?"
A1. Because with a strictly lossy round trip (eta_c*eta_d < 1), charging and
    discharging in the same period routes energy through a loss for no net grid
    benefit; a cost minimizer never pays to waste energy, so an optimal solution
    with min(charge,discharge)=0 always exists and the XOR flag forbids only
    what's already avoided. The one load-bearing assumption is that nothing in
    the objective/constraints REWARDS throughput or wasting energy (no cycling
    payment, no "paid to consume" negative-price sink). Drop that, or set
    eta_rt=1, and the guarantee fails.

Q2. "Your solver returns charge[t]=5 and discharge[t]=5 in the same hour with
     eta_rt=0.85. What happened and what do you do?"
A2. With eta_rt<1 strictly this should be cost-suboptimal, so three suspects:
    (i) a MODELING ERROR — some term rewards gross flow (wrong-signed
    degradation credit, ancillary/mileage payment, gross-import fee), i.e. a
    real Break; (ii) NEGATIVE PRICES giving a paid energy sink; or (iii) you're
    on an ALTERNATIVE OPTIMUM / numerical tolerance and the loop is cost-neutral
    noise. Diagnose: recompute the objective with the loop netted out
    (max(c-d,0), max(d-c,0)); if cost is unchanged it's degeneracy -> net it in
    post-processing or add an epsilon*(charge+discharge) tie-breaker. If netting
    it out REDUCES cost, your model is wrong somewhere and you hunt the
    flow-rewarding term. If it's genuinely optimal to loop, one of the Breaks is
    real and you need the binary.

Q3. "Where do the efficiencies go in the SOC balance, and why does misplacing
     them matter?"
A3. SOC[t] = SOC[t-1] + eta_c*charge[t]*dt - (discharge[t]/eta_d)*dt. Charging is
    MULTIPLIED by eta_c (only a fraction of drawn energy is stored); discharging
    is DIVIDED by eta_d (you must pull MORE from the battery than you deliver).
    Both encode loss. If you instead multiplied discharge by eta_d (or divided
    charge by eta_c) you'd get the signs of the loss wrong — the model could show
    the battery delivering more than it stored, i.e. free energy, and the whole
    arbitrage economics (and the no-loop proof, which hinges on 1/eta_d > eta_c)
    would be invalid. Bonus: round-trip loss shows up as eta_c*eta_d; e.g.
    eta_c=eta_d=0.92 -> eta_rt~0.846, ~15% lost per full cycle.

Bonus Q (often asked): "Why the terminal SOC constraint?"
   Over a finite horizon, with nothing forcing an end state the LP will drain the
   battery in the last hours (free revenue, no future need) — an artifact, not a
   strategy. Fix SOC[T]=SOC_0 (cyclic) or SOC[T]>=target so the plan is
   steady-state-meaningful and comparable across runs/years.
