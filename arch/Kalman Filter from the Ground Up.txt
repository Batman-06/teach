===========================================================================
THE KALMAN FILTER FROM THE GROUND UP  — math + intuition, ML/forecasting POV
===========================================================================

Audience: ML/DS person doing forecasting who keeps seeing "Kalman filter"
mentioned. Goal: understand it well enough to USE it on time series, read a
state-space forecasting library, and reason about it — not just recite formulas.

Prereq that helps (already in your notes): the STATE-VARIABLE idea from
"LP & Convexity Basics + Multi-Period State Coupling.txt" — x_t = f(x_{t-1}, ...)
is a state that carries info forward. The Kalman filter is the same state idea,
but PROBABILISTIC (Gaussian) and the state is HIDDEN (you only see noisy
measurements of it). If you know that note's RNN / state-space analogy, you're
already oriented.

Other background assumed: Gaussian distributions, mean/variance, basic matrix
algebra (multiply, transpose, inverse). That's it.


===========================================================================
PART 0 — THE ONE-SENTENCE IDEA
===========================================================================

You have a system whose true state you can't see directly; you have (a) a model
of how the state evolves and (b) noisy measurements of it. The Kalman filter
optimally FUSES "what my model predicts" with "what I just measured", weighting
each by how much you trust it, and does so RECURSIVELY — one cheap update per
time step, no need to store history.

Two-line mental model:
      new estimate = prediction + GAIN * (measurement - prediction)
      the GAIN = (how uncertain is my prediction) / (prediction + measurement uncertainty)
Trust the measurement more when your prediction is shaky; trust the prediction
more when the sensor is noisy. The filter computes that trade-off optimally.


===========================================================================
PART 1 — THE PROBLEM IT SOLVES (intuition first)
===========================================================================

Imagine tracking the "true underlying demand level" of a time series. You can't
observe it cleanly — every reading is corrupted by noise (metering error,
weather blips, one-off events). You also have a belief about how the level
moves hour to hour (e.g. "it drifts slowly"). Each new data point you face a
question: is this jump real signal, or just noise? How much should I move my
estimate?

   - Trust every measurement fully -> your estimate is as noisy as the data.
   - Ignore measurements, trust the model -> you drift away from reality.

The Kalman filter is the principled answer: it maintains not just an ESTIMATE
but an UNCERTAINTY (variance) about that estimate, and uses both uncertainties
to decide the blend. It is the optimal linear estimator, and under Gaussian
noise it's the optimal estimator period (equals the Bayesian posterior mean).


===========================================================================
PART 2 — THE STATE-SPACE MODEL (the two equations everything rests on)
===========================================================================

The Kalman filter assumes your system is described by a LINEAR-GAUSSIAN state-
space model. Two equations:

(1) PROCESS / TRANSITION model — how the hidden state evolves:

      x_k = F x_{k-1} + B u_k + w_k ,      w_k ~ N(0, Q)

(2) MEASUREMENT / OBSERVATION model — how what you SEE relates to the state:

      z_k = H x_k + v_k ,                  v_k ~ N(0, R)

Dictionary (dimensions matter — this is where people get lost):
   x_k   (n x 1)  the HIDDEN STATE at time k (what you want to know). e.g.
                  [level, trend] of a demand series.
   z_k   (m x 1)  the MEASUREMENT you actually observe at time k.
   F     (n x n)  state-transition matrix — your dynamics model.
   B, u_k         optional control input (external known driver). Drop if none.
   H     (m x n)  observation matrix — maps hidden state to measurement space.
   w_k            PROCESS NOISE — randomness in the dynamics. Covariance Q (nxn).
   v_k            MEASUREMENT NOISE — sensor/observation error. Covariance R (mxm).
   Q     (n x n)  how much you DON'T trust your own dynamics model.
   R     (m x m)  how much you DON'T trust your measurements.

Everything is Gaussian, so a "belief about the state" is fully described by just
a MEAN vector and a COVARIANCE matrix:
   x_hat_{k|j}  = estimate of x_k given data up to time j
   P_{k|j}      = covariance (uncertainty) of that estimate
Notation: "k|k-1" = PREDICTED (prior, before seeing z_k);
          "k|k"   = UPDATED  (posterior, after seeing z_k).

KEY MODELING INSIGHT: F, H, Q, R ARE your modeling choices. Picking them is how
you encode "it's a slow-drifting level" vs "level + linear trend" vs "seasonal."
Part 7 shows the forecasting choices.


===========================================================================
PART 3 — THE RECURSION: PREDICT, THEN UPDATE
===========================================================================

Each time step does two phases. This is the whole algorithm.

------------------------- PREDICT (time update) -------------------------
Push the previous estimate forward through the dynamics (no new data yet):

   x_hat_{k|k-1} = F x_hat_{k-1|k-1} + B u_k                       (3)
   P_{k|k-1}     = F P_{k-1|k-1} F^T + Q                           (4)

Read it:
   (3) move the mean forward by the dynamics.
   (4) move the uncertainty forward: F P F^T propagates existing uncertainty
       through the dynamics, and + Q ADDS fresh uncertainty because the process
       itself is noisy. Uncertainty ALWAYS GROWS during prediction (that's why
       forecasts get less certain the further out you go).

------------------------- UPDATE (measurement update) -------------------
Now a measurement z_k arrives. Correct the prediction:

   INNOVATION (surprise):          y_k = z_k - H x_hat_{k|k-1}      (5)
   INNOVATION COVARIANCE:          S_k = H P_{k|k-1} H^T + R        (6)
   KALMAN GAIN:                    K_k = P_{k|k-1} H^T S_k^{-1}     (7)
   STATE UPDATE:      x_hat_{k|k}  = x_hat_{k|k-1} + K_k y_k        (8)
   COVARIANCE UPDATE: P_{k|k}      = (I - K_k H) P_{k|k-1}          (9)

Read it:
   (5) the INNOVATION is "actual measurement minus what I predicted I'd see" —
       the new information, the part you did NOT already expect.
   (6) S_k is how uncertain that prediction-of-the-measurement was: prediction
       uncertainty mapped into measurement space (H P H^T) plus sensor noise R.
   (7) the GAIN is the crux (Part 4).
   (8) nudge the estimate toward the measurement, scaled by the gain.
   (9) seeing data REDUCES uncertainty: P shrinks in the update. Predict grows
       it, update shrinks it — the filter breathes.

That's it. Initialize with x_hat_{0|0}, P_{0|0}, then loop predict->update
forever. O(n^3) per step (a matrix inverse), independent of history length —
this recursiveness is the whole engineering appeal.


===========================================================================
PART 4 — THE KALMAN GAIN: the heart of it
===========================================================================

K_k = P_{k|k-1} H^T S_k^{-1}  =  (prediction uncertainty) /
                                 (prediction uncertainty + measurement noise)
(the division is the scalar intuition; in matrix form it's * S^{-1}).

Behavior at the two extremes (scalar case H=1 makes it crystal clear,
K = P_pred / (P_pred + R)):
   - MEASUREMENT very noisy (R -> infinity):  K -> 0. Ignore the measurement,
     keep the prediction. ("My sensor is garbage, trust the model.")
   - PREDICTION very uncertain (P_pred -> infinity):  K -> 1. Jump to the
     measurement, throw away the prediction. ("I have no idea, so whatever I
     just measured is my best guess.")
   - In between: a weighted blend, with weight set by RELATIVE uncertainty.

So the gain is an automatic, self-tuning trust dial between model and data. You
never hand-tune a smoothing constant — the filter derives it each step from the
uncertainties you declared via Q and R. (And as Part 7 shows, at steady state
this gain becomes exactly the alpha of exponential smoothing.)


===========================================================================
PART 5 — WHERE THE EQUATIONS COME FROM (two derivations)
===========================================================================

You can derive the exact same update two ways; pick whichever clicks.

5.1 BAYESIAN view (cleanest for an ML person)
---------------------------------------------
Everything is Gaussian, and Gaussians are closed under the operations we need:
   - PRIOR (from predict):   x_k ~ N(x_hat_{k|k-1}, P_{k|k-1})
   - LIKELIHOOD (measurement): z_k | x_k ~ N(H x_k, R)
   - POSTERIOR (Bayes):      p(x_k | z_k) proportional to prior * likelihood
Product of two Gaussians is Gaussian, and its mean/covariance are EXACTLY
equations (8)-(9). So the Kalman update IS Bayesian inference for a linear-
Gaussian model: posterior = prior fused with likelihood. The Kalman gain is
just the algebra of combining two Gaussian "opinions" weighted by their
precisions (inverse variances). This is the same precision-weighting you'd see
in Gaussian Bayesian linear regression.

5.2 Least-squares / BLUE view (no Gaussian assumption needed)
-------------------------------------------------------------
Drop the Gaussian assumption. Ask only: among all estimators that are LINEAR in
the measurement and UNBIASED, which minimizes the mean-squared error (the trace
of P_{k|k})? Answer: the Kalman gain (7). So the KF is the Best Linear Unbiased
Estimator (BLUE) regardless of distribution; Gaussianity only upgrades it from
"best linear" to "best overall." You get optimality either way, which is why it
is so widely trusted.

(Derivation of the gain, scalar sketch: minimize P_post = (1-K)^2 P_pred + K^2 R
over K; d/dK = -2(1-K)P_pred + 2K R = 0 -> K = P_pred/(P_pred+R). Matrix case is
the same idea with traces and matrix calculus.)


===========================================================================
PART 6 — WORKED NUMERIC EXAMPLE (scalar, do it by hand)
===========================================================================

Estimate a true (roughly constant) demand level from noisy hourly readings.
Scalar model: F=1 (level persists), H=1 (we measure the level directly),
process noise Q=0 (assume truly constant for this demo), measurement noise
variance R=4 (sensor std = 2). Diffuse initial guess: x0=0, P0=1000 (we know
almost nothing).

Scalar equations:
   predict: x_pred = x_prev ;  P_pred = P_prev + Q
   update:  K = P_pred/(P_pred+R)
            x = x_pred + K*(z - x_pred)
            P = (1-K)*P_pred

Measurements arrive: z1=10, z2=12, z3=9.

STEP 1  (z1 = 10):
   predict: x_pred=0, P_pred=1000+0=1000
   K = 1000/(1000+4) = 0.996
   x = 0 + 0.996*(10-0) = 9.96
   P = (1-0.996)*1000 = 3.98
   -> almost jumped straight to the measurement (K~1) because we started
      clueless (P huge). Makes sense.

STEP 2  (z2 = 12):
   predict: x_pred=9.96, P_pred=3.98
   K = 3.98/(3.98+4) = 0.4987
   x = 9.96 + 0.4987*(12-9.96) = 9.96 + 1.017 = 10.98
   P = (1-0.4987)*3.98 = 1.995
   -> now K~0.5: we trust the new reading about as much as our estimate, so we
      move halfway. Uncertainty keeps dropping.

STEP 3  (z3 = 9):
   predict: x_pred=10.98, P_pred=1.995
   K = 1.995/(1.995+4) = 0.3328
   x = 10.98 + 0.3328*(9-10.98) = 10.98 - 0.659 = 10.32
   P = (1-0.3328)*1.995 = 1.331
   -> K~0.33 now: estimate is getting confident, so the low reading of 9 only
      pulls it down modestly. The filter is "settling."

Notice the gain SHRINKS each step (0.996 -> 0.499 -> 0.333). With Q=0 and a
constant, the Kalman filter is EXACTLY the running sample mean: after n readings
K_n = 1/(n+1)-style weighting and x converges to the average. That's a great
sanity anchor: KF of a constant = recursive averaging.

Now flip ONE knob to see dynamics: set Q>0 (say Q=1). Then P_pred = P_prev + Q
never decays to ~0, so K settles at a positive floor and the filter stays
PERMANENTLY responsive to new data — it "forgets" old data and tracks a moving
level instead of averaging a constant. Q is literally the "how fast does the
world change / how much should I forget" knob.


===========================================================================
PART 7 — KALMAN FILTERS FOR FORECASTING (your actual use case)
===========================================================================

State-space models are a backbone of modern time-series forecasting (they
underlie statsmodels' UnobservedComponents, Facebook-style structural models,
BSTS, and the exponential-smoothing family). The KF is the engine that runs
them. Three things it gives a forecaster:

7.1 Common model templates (just choices of F, H, Q, R)
-------------------------------------------------------
   - LOCAL LEVEL (random walk + noise): x = level; F=1, H=1.
         level_k = level_{k-1} + w,  z_k = level_k + v.
         Forecast = last filtered level, flat. KF steady state == SIMPLE
         EXPONENTIAL SMOOTHING, with alpha = the steady-state Kalman gain. This
         is the exact bridge between KF and the smoothing you already know.
   - LOCAL LINEAR TREND: state = [level, slope].
         F = [[1,1],[0,1]], H=[1,0]. Captures trend; forecast extends the slope.
         Equivalent to Holt's linear method at steady state.
   - SEASONAL / STRUCTURAL: stack level + trend + seasonal dummies into the
     state vector; F encodes how each component evolves. This is a "structural
     time series" — interpretable components, each with its own noise.

7.2 Forecasting = iterate the PREDICT step with no updates
----------------------------------------------------------
To forecast h steps ahead, run the predict equations (3)-(4) forward h times
WITHOUT any update (no future measurements exist). The mean gives the point
forecast; P_{k+h|k} grows with each step and gives you a PRINCIPLED, WIDENING
PREDICTION INTERVAL for free. That expanding uncertainty cone is a direct,
honest output — not a bolt-on.

7.3 Parameter estimation via the likelihood
-------------------------------------------
You usually don't know Q, R (and maybe F) exactly. The KF produces the
innovations y_k and their covariances S_k, and the Gaussian log-likelihood of
the data is a simple sum over them (the "prediction error decomposition"):
      log L = -1/2 * sum_k [ log|S_k| + y_k^T S_k^{-1} y_k ] + const
Maximize this over the unknown parameters (grad descent / EM) to FIT the model.
So the KF isn't just a filter — it's how you compute the likelihood to train
state-space forecasters. This is the piece most relevant if you're choosing
between a KF/structural model and, say, an ML regressor.

7.4 Why a forecaster might choose it
------------------------------------
   - Handles missing data naturally (just skip the update step — predict only).
   - Online / streaming: O(1)-per-step updates, no refit on new data.
   - Decomposes a series into interpretable level/trend/seasonal states.
   - Gives calibrated uncertainty, not just point forecasts.
   - Few parameters, works on short series where a deep model would overfit.
Trade-off: it assumes LINEAR dynamics + GAUSSIAN noise. Nonlinear or heavy-
tailed? See extensions.


===========================================================================
PART 8 — WHEN LINEAR-GAUSSIAN BREAKS: THE EXTENSIONS (map, not detail)
===========================================================================

   - EXTENDED KF (EKF): dynamics/measurement are NONLINEAR, x_k = f(x_{k-1}),
     z_k = h(x_k). Linearize f, h via their Jacobians at the current estimate,
     then apply the normal KF. Workhorse in robotics/GPS; can diverge if the
     nonlinearity is strong or the Jacobian is a poor local fit.
   - UNSCENTED KF (UKF): instead of linearizing, push a few deterministic
     "sigma points" through the true nonlinear function and refit a Gaussian.
     More accurate than EKF for strong nonlinearity, no Jacobians needed.
   - PARTICLE FILTER: drop Gaussianity entirely; represent the belief with a
     cloud of weighted samples. Handles arbitrary nonlinear/non-Gaussian/
     multimodal problems; cost is many particles (Monte Carlo).
   - KALMAN SMOOTHER (RTS): the filter uses only PAST data (x_{k|k}). If you're
     doing offline analysis and want the best estimate at time k using ALL data
     including the future (x_{k|T}), run a backward pass — the RTS smoother.
     Use filter for real-time, smoother for retrospective/backtesting.

Choosing: linear+Gaussian -> KF; mildly nonlinear -> EKF/UKF; nasty/multimodal
-> particle filter; offline best-estimate -> add the smoother.


===========================================================================
PART 9 — HOW IT RELATES TO THINGS YOU ALREADY KNOW
===========================================================================

   - EXPONENTIAL SMOOTHING: the steady-state KF of a local-level model IS simple
     exponential smoothing; Holt/Holt-Winters are KFs of trend/seasonal models.
     KF is the general, uncertainty-aware parent.
   - RECURSIVE LEAST SQUARES (RLS): KF with static state (F=I, Q=0) is exactly
     RLS / online Bayesian linear regression — the Part 6 "running mean" result.
   - RNN / STATE-SPACE NETS: same hidden-state recurrence h_k=f(h_{k-1},x_k),
     but an RNN LEARNS the transition and is deterministic/nonlinear; the KF has
     a KNOWN linear transition and tracks a full probability distribution
     (mean+covariance), giving calibrated uncertainty an RNN doesn't by default.
   - HIDDEN MARKOV MODELS: an HMM is the discrete-state cousin; the KF is the
     continuous-Gaussian analog. The predict/update recursion mirrors the HMM
     forward algorithm.
   - BAYESIAN FILTERING: the KF is the exact closed-form solution of the general
     Bayes filter (predict = Chapman-Kolmogorov, update = Bayes rule) in the
     special case where everything is linear-Gaussian.


===========================================================================
PART 9.5 — WHERE KALMAN FILTERS ACTUALLY SHOW UP ACROSS ML & DS (a catalog)
===========================================================================

Part 9 was about conceptual EQUIVALENCES. This is the APPLICATION landscape —
the concrete places you'll run into KFs (or should reach for one) in ML/DS, so
you recognize them in papers, libraries, and job interviews.

(A) TIME-SERIES FORECASTING & STRUCTURAL MODELS  (your case — Part 7).
    statsmodels UnobservedComponents, BSTS, dynamic linear models (DLMs). The KF
    runs the model and computes the likelihood for fitting. Most "state-space
    forecasting" in a DS stack is a Kalman filter under the hood.

(B) OBJECT TRACKING / COMPUTER VISION  (probably the #1 place ML engineers meet
    it). Multi-object trackers SORT and DeepSORT use a Kalman filter per object
    to predict bounding-box position/velocity between detections and smooth the
    track; the detector gives noisy measurements, the KF fills gaps and handles
    missed frames. Also point/feature tracking and radar/lidar tracking.

(C) ROBOTICS, SLAM & SENSOR FUSION. EKF-SLAM for simultaneous localization and
    mapping; fusing GPS + IMU + wheel odometry into one pose estimate (classic
    sensor fusion). Drones, self-driving, AR headsets all lean on EKF/UKF here.

(D) FINANCE & ECONOMETRICS. Time-varying parameter models: estimating a
    DYNAMIC hedge ratio / rolling beta (CAPM beta that drifts), pairs-trading
    spread estimation, dynamic factor models, stochastic-volatility filtering.
    "Let the regression coefficient be a hidden state that random-walks" is a
    very common KF pattern in quant DS.

(E) REINFORCEMENT LEARNING & OPTIMAL CONTROL. LQG control = LQR (optimal control)
    + Kalman filter (optimal state estimation), tied by the SEPARATION PRINCIPLE
    (estimate the state, then control as if it were known). State estimation in
    POMDPs, belief tracking, Kalman Temporal Differences, and some model-based
    RL use KF machinery.

(F) TRAINING NEURAL NETWORKS (the one people forget). The EXTENDED KALMAN FILTER
    was a classic method for training NN weights: treat the weights as a hidden
    state to be estimated, measurements are the targets — a second-order-ish
    online learner. Related modern threads: Kalman-/natural-gradient-flavored
    optimizers and online Bayesian weight updates. Conceptually, an RLS/KF view
    sits behind several adaptive optimizers.

(G) GAUSSIAN PROCESSES made FAST. A GP on 1-D (time) with certain kernels
    (Matern, and others with a state-space representation) is EXACTLY a linear-
    Gaussian state-space model. Running a Kalman filter+smoother does GP
    regression in O(n) instead of the naive O(n^3) — a major scalability trick
    for long time series. If you like GPs, this is the KF connection that pays.

(H) DEEP STATE-SPACE / DEEP GENERATIVE MODELS. Deep Kalman Filters, the Kalman
    Variational Autoencoder (KVAE), structured/variational inference networks,
    and modern deep SSMs (the S4/Mamba family descends from state-space thinking)
    all build on the predict/update + Gaussian-state idea, often with learned or
    nonlinear transitions and amortized inference.

(I) CAUSAL INFERENCE / IMPACT MEASUREMENT. Bayesian structural time series power
    Google's CausalImpact: fit a state-space model on the pre-intervention
    series, forecast the counterfactual, and attribute the gap to the
    intervention. The KF is the inference engine.

(J) DATA ASSIMILATION AT SCALE. The ENSEMBLE Kalman Filter (EnKF) fuses models
    with observations in weather forecasting, climate, oceanography, epidemiology
    — very high-dimensional states where you propagate an ensemble instead of a
    full covariance. The large-scale cousin of the plain KF.

(K) ANOMALY DETECTION & MONITORING. The INNOVATION y_k (measurement minus
    prediction) and its covariance S_k give a normalized residual; a large
    innovation = "the world surprised my model" = an anomaly/change-point signal.
    Monitoring innovations (e.g. CUSUM on them) is a clean online detector, and
    directly relevant to flagging bad data or regime shifts in a forecasting
    pipeline.

(L) ONLINE LEARNING / CONCEPT DRIFT. Track a slowly-changing parameter by making
    it a random-walk state; the KF's Q knob sets how fast you "forget" old data
    (Part 6). A principled alternative to a fixed sliding window or hand-tuned
    decay.

(M) PROBABILISTIC GRAPHICAL MODELS. The KF is Gaussian belief propagation /
    message passing on a chain-structured factor graph — the continuous-Gaussian
    analog of the HMM forward-backward algorithm. Useful framing if you come at
    it from the PGM side.

One-line map for recognizing it in the wild: whenever someone has a HIDDEN,
EVOLVING quantity observed through NOISE and wants an online, uncertainty-aware
estimate — tracking, fusion, drifting parameters, counterfactual series — a
Kalman filter (or a nonlinear/ensemble/deep variant) is very likely the tool,
named or not.


===========================================================================
PART 10 — THREE INTERVIEW QUESTIONS (with answers)
===========================================================================

Q1. "What does the Kalman gain actually represent, and what happens as
     measurement noise R goes to zero vs infinity?"
A1. It's the optimal weight on the new measurement's innovation — the ratio of
    prediction uncertainty to total (prediction + measurement) uncertainty,
    K = P_pred H^T (H P_pred H^T + R)^{-1}. As R->0 (perfect sensor) K-> (pseudo)
    inverse of H, i.e. trust the measurement fully and effectively reset the
    estimate to it; uncertainty collapses. As R->infinity (useless sensor) K->0,
    ignore the measurement and keep the model prediction. It is a self-tuning
    trust dial between model and data set by relative uncertainty.

Q2. "Why does the covariance P grow in one phase and shrink in the other?"
A2. Two phases. PREDICT propagates the old uncertainty through the dynamics and
    ADDS process noise Q: P_pred = F P F^T + Q, so uncertainty GROWS — time
    passing without data makes you less sure (this is what widens multi-step
    forecasts). UPDATE incorporates a measurement: P_post = (I-KH)P_pred, which
    SHRINKS it — information reduces uncertainty. A running filter alternates
    grow/shrink; if it reaches equilibrium the two balance and P (and the gain)
    converge to a steady state.

Q3. "I give you a time series with gaps and I want forecasts with uncertainty
     bands. How does a Kalman/state-space approach handle both, and what's its
     core assumption?"
A3. Missing data: on a gap, run ONLY the predict step (no measurement to update
    with) — P simply grows across the gap, which is exactly correct (you're less
    sure where there's no data). Forecasting: iterate predict h steps past the
    last observation; the mean is the point forecast and the growing P_{k+h|k}
    gives honest, widening prediction intervals directly. Core assumption:
    linear dynamics with additive GAUSSIAN process and measurement noise (and
    that you've specified F,H,Q,R, usually fitting Q,R by maximizing the
    innovation-based likelihood). If dynamics are strongly nonlinear or noise is
    heavy-tailed/multimodal, you move to EKF/UKF or a particle filter.

Bonus Q: "Filter vs smoother?" The FILTER estimates x_k using data up to k
(causal, real-time). The SMOOTHER (RTS) uses the WHOLE series including future
points to refine each x_k (offline, lower variance). Use filter live, smoother
for retrospective analysis/backtesting.
